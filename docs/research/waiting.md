# Designing the 30-120 s wait: what makes a long AI generation feel shorter and useful

Research note, 2026-10-07. All web sources accessed 2026-10-07 unless stated. "Read" means I read the primary source myself (for the papers, the full PDF); "unverified" means I could only confirm it through a search summary or a secondary source.

Scope: the screen a learner sees between submitting a question and part 1 of the lesson arriving (`frontend/src/components/LiveLesson.tsx`, the `blueprint` header branch). Measured stages: routing about 3 s, planning about 30-50 s with no visible output until it ends, then part 1 written in about 20-30 s. The dead zone is roughly the first 35-80 s.

## Executive summary: top recommendations

1. **Replace the pulsing dot with a figure being drawn on the blueprint grid.** A marigold "pen" plots axes, points, a curve, bars: the lesson's own visual vocabulary. It answers Maister's first proposition, "Occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time" ([Maister](https://www.columbia.edu/~ww2040/4615S13/Psychology_of_Waiting_Lines.pdf)). It also replaces the looped indicator NN/g reserves for 2-10 s waits ([Sherwin, NN/g 2014](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)).
2. **Narrate the real work, honestly.** Show a short list of what the planner is deciding (the parts, the key ideas, the words you will meet), and tick an item only when a real event arrives. Showing the work raised perceived value even when it made the wait longer ([Buell & Norton 2011](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Norton_Michael_The%20labor%20illusion%20How%20operational_f4269b70-3732-4fc4-8113-72d0c47533e0.pdf)). Never tick on a timer.
3. **Ask one warm-up prediction question during planning.** Guessing before learning improves later recall of the guessed material, even when the guess is wrong ([Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kornell/Publications/Kornell.Hays.Bjork.2009.pdf); [Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf)). The question must target something part 1 actually teaches, because the benefit did not spread to untested items. Close the loop in the lesson ("You guessed B. Here is why...").
4. **Add a time-based progress rail that accelerates and never stalls near the end.** Harrison et al. found accelerating bars felt faster and pauses near the end felt slower ([2007](https://www.chrisharrison.net/index.php/Research/ProgressBars)), and backwards-moving ribbing cut perceived duration by 11% ([2010](https://www.chrisharrison.net/index.php/Research/ProgressBars2)). Drive the rail from measured stage medians, not real percent-done, and say so to assistive tech.
5. **Show elapsed time, not a countdown.** In a 2026 study of 10-60 s delays, a remaining-time display raised frustration compared with elapsed time, and no time display made waits feel longer ([Tan & Nov 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04138)). Keep "23 s", and set the expectation in words up front ("about a minute").
6. **Once the outline lands, turn the wait into a preview.** Split the figure into 3 or 4 "plates", one per part, with titles, and show the key points and glossary as reading material. This mirrors the plan-first and interim-deliverable patterns of Gemini Deep Research and the guidance in [Nielsen 2025](https://www.uxtigers.com/post/progress-indicators). Maister's restaurant menu is the same move.
7. **Hand off to part 1 without yanking the learner.** Auto-open part 1 when it arrives, unless the learner is mid-answer on the warm-up; then show a "Part 1 is ready" button instead. Flag the tab title so a learner who wandered off sees it ([Nielsen 2025](https://www.uxtigers.com/post/progress-indicators)).
8. **Fix the live region now.** The current `aria-live="polite"` region contains `${status}, ${seconds} s`, so the text changes every second. Move the seconds counter out of the live region, put `role="status"` on the stage line, and announce stage changes only ([WCAG 4.1.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/status-messages.html) warns against "chatty" live regions).
9. **Give the drawing a Pause control and a static reduced-motion form.** The animation runs for more than 5 s alongside interactive content (the warm-up), so the WCAG 2.2.2 preload exemption is doubtful ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). Under `prefers-reduced-motion`, show finished figures that cross-fade.
10. **Skip skeleton screens for this wait.** NN/g scopes them to waits under 10 s ([Tankala, NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)), and the one controlled comparison I could read found skeletons perceived as *slowest* ([Viget 2017](https://www.viget.com/articles/a-bone-to-pick-with-skeleton-screens)).

## 1. Response-time limits and progress indicators

- **The three limits.** 0.1 s "is about the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously". 1 s is the limit "for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted". 10 s "is about the limit for keeping the user's attention focused on the dialogue" ([Nielsen 1993](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)). Past 10 s, "users should be given feedback indicating when the computer expects to be done", which matters most when response time is "highly variable". Our planning stage (30-50 s) is both long and variable.
- **Looped vs percent-done.** NN/g recommends a looped animation for 2-10 s and a percent-done indicator past 10 s. It also says a percent-done animation should start slower and accelerate toward the end, and that a text explanation should go with the indicator ([Sherwin 2014](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)). The same article cites a University of Nebraska-Lincoln study in which people who saw a moving bar "were willing to wait on average 3 times longer". I did not trace that study to its original, so the 3x figure is **unverified**.
- **Agentic waits.** Nielsen's 2025 update for AI agents recommends five things: the plan as a live step list, narration in user-meaningful units, interim deliverables, elapsed time always shown (remaining time only when reliable), and a completion notification. Past about a minute, he advises assuming the user has left ([Nielsen 2025](https://www.uxtigers.com/post/progress-indicators)). Our 35-80 s sits right on that line, so the screen should hold attention *and* survive a tab switch.
- **Elapsed vs remaining.** 425 participants waited 10, 30 or 60 s with a remaining-time display, an elapsed-time display, or none. "Remaining-Time feedback increased frustration relative to Elapsed-Time feedback, while No Time Display made waits feel longer". None of the three changed task performance after the wait ([Tan & Nov 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04138), preprint, abstract only read).

## 2. Labor illusion and operational transparency

- **The finding (read).** Buell and Norton replaced a progress bar with "a running tally of the tasks being performed": the airlines being searched, the dating profiles being scanned. People "can actually prefer websites with longer waits to those that return instantaneous results - even when those results are identical" ([Management Science 57(9), 2011, pp. 1564-1579](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376); [author PDF](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Norton_Michael_The%20labor%20illusion%20How%20operational_f4269b70-3732-4fc4-8113-72d0c47533e0.pdf)). In Experiment 2, people who saw the tally preferred the slower site to the instant one at 30 s (62%) and 60 s (63%). Without the tally, only 42% did at 30 s and 23% at 60 s. The mechanism is perceived effort and reciprocity, not extra information.
- **The boundary condition (read).** Experiment 5: transparency plus a *bad* result produced the worst ratings of all. "No amount of effort can overcome consumers' natural inclination to dislike services that perform poorly." For us, the narrated wait raises the bar for the lesson; it cannot paper over a weak one.
- **Later work.** Buell, Kim and Tsay found that visually revealing the process to consumers, and the consumers to the producers, creates value for both sides ([Management Science 63(6), 2017](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=50173)). I read the abstract only. Buell's 2021 government study extends the effect to trust ([MSOM](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/msom.2020.0877); not read, **unverified** detail).
- **Implication.** Name the planner's real outputs (parts, key ideas, words) while it works. Ticks must follow real events: the experiments show the effect, but faking completion would be a lie to a learner, and the `plan` event already tells us when planning is done.

## 3. Perceived duration and the shape of progress

- **Pacing (read).** Harrison, Amento, Kuznetsov and Bell compared nine progress functions over equal durations. Only Power and Fast Power, "with the fastest progress occurring near the end", were perceived as faster than linear. Every function with a pause near the end was perceived as slower: "participants perceived progress bars with pauses as taking longer", and both effects were stronger "towards the end of the process" ([UIST 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1294211.1294231); [PDF via author page](https://www.chrisharrison.net/index.php/Research/ProgressBars)).
- **Visual augmentation (read).** Harrison, Yeo and Hudson: "Progress bars with animated ribbing that move backwards in a decelerating manner proved to have the strongest effect", reducing perceived duration by 11%. Pulsation with increasing frequency was also preferred over constant or decreasing frequency ([CHI 2010](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1753326.1753556); [author page](https://www.chrisharrison.net/index.php/Research/ProgressBars2)).
- **Maister (read, 2005 reprint of the 1985 essay).** The relevant propositions: "Occupied Time Feels Shorter Than Unoccupied Time"; "preprocess waits are perceived as longer than in-process waits"; "Uncertain Waits Are Longer than Known, Finite Waits"; "Unexplained Waits Are Longer than Explained Waits" ([PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/~ww2040/4615S13/Psychology_of_Waiting_Lines.pdf)). The menu example matters most: it fills time *and* gets the customer ready to order. A preview of the lesson's key points does both.
- **Implication for the rail.** Time-base it on stage medians (planning about 40 s). Run a power-curve fill that reaches about 80% at the median, then creeps rather than stalls. At 1.5x the median, switch the copy to "Taking longer than usual" instead of freezing at 99%. That is an extrapolation from the two papers, not a tested design.

## 4. Skeleton screens vs spinners

- NN/g: use skeletons for full-page loads of 2-10 s, and progress bars past 10 s. Avoid frame-only skeletons. Shimmer can be "distracting, annoying, or even create accessibility problems" ([Tankala 2023, reviewed 2026-09-02](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)).
- Evidence is thin and mixed. Mejtoft, Långström and Söderström report skeletons perceived as faster ([ECCE 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3232078.3232086); abstract only, **unverified** effect size). Viget's 136-person test found the opposite: perceived wait was 2.82 s for the skeleton, 2.41 s for the spinner and 2.29 s for a blank screen ([Faulkner & Olvera 2017](https://www.viget.com/articles/a-bone-to-pick-with-skeleton-screens)). The post reports no significance test that I could see, and the authors say skeletons may "work best when wait times are very short". A street-intercept study by Bill Chung favouring a slow left-to-right shimmer is reported only in secondary sources (**unverified**).
- Implication: a skeleton of a lesson we cannot yet shape is a 40 s grey box. Use the drawing instead, then real titles once the outline exists.

## 6. Occupying the wait with learning: pretesting

- **Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009 (read).** Participants answered fictional or weak-associate questions they could not get right; the trials they guessed correctly were excluded. "Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhanced learning", compared with seeing the question and answer together ([JEP: LMC 35(4), 989-998](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kornell/Publications/Kornell.Hays.Bjork.2009.pdf)).
- **Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009 (read).** Across 5 experiments, students pretested on an essay's concepts beat students given extra reading time, "even though only items that were not successfully retrieved on the pretest were analyzed". Experiment 5 showed the questions without asking for answers, to separate testing from attention direction. "The benefit of testing did not spread to untested items" ([JEP: Applied 15(3), 243-257](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf)).
- **Implication.** The warm-up must ask about a concept the lesson will answer, and the answer must come soon after. Two sources are possible. (a) The router, already a model call, also emits one prediction question with 3 options. The planner is then told to answer it in part 1. This is a backend change, and it shows the question at about 3 s. (b) A deterministic template ("Before you read: what do you think happens to X when...?") filled from the question. This option is free but weaker. Either way, the answer is free-choice and ungraded, and part 1 closes the loop. Whether a 30 s, single-item pretest replicates the lab effect is **unverified**; it is cheap to A/B test.

## 7. Proposed design

One SVG on the existing `.blueprint` field, beside or under the question heading. The pen is a 6 px marigold (`--marker`) dot; the lines are white at 85% opacity. Easing is `cubic-bezier(.2,.7,.2,1)` for draws and linear for the pen's travel. Sketches are seeded from a hash of the question, so the same question always draws the same figures.

| Stage | On screen | What animates, and timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Routing** (0-3 s) | Question heading; the line "Reading your question"; elapsed seconds; the line "Usually about a minute" | The x axis draws in (500 ms), then the y axis (500 ms), then ticks fade in with a 30 ms stagger. The rail starts. Nothing else. |
| **Planning** (3 s to the `plan` event) | The drawing; a 3-item work list: "Choosing the parts", "Picking the key ideas", "Collecting the words you'll meet", each with a hollow marker; the warm-up question card; the rail; elapsed time | **Sketch loop**, 8-10 s per sketch, cycling through: a scatter (points pop in, 60 ms stagger), a fitted line drawn by the pen (1.2 s), bars growing from the baseline with a 2 s hold, and a curve traced with an annotation ring. Between sketches, the old one fades to 25% (400 ms) and stays as a faint layer, so the grid fills up like a working page. The pen pulse speeds up gently over each sketch (after Harrison 2010; an extrapolation). **Rail:** a power-curve fill to about 80% at 40 s, with faint ribbing drifting backwards. At 60 s the copy switches to "Taking longer than usual". |
| **Outline known** (the `plan` event to part 1) | The work list ticks all three items at once (150 ms apart). The drawing slides into 3 or 4 **plates**, small framed thumbnails titled with the part names. Plate 1 says "Drawing now" and the rest say "Queued". Below: the key points as a numbered preview and the glossary as chips ("Words you'll meet"). The warm-up stays if unanswered. | Plates enter with a 600 ms morph from the full figure (one transition, after [Heer & Robertson](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf), cited in `scrollytelling.md`). Only plate 1 keeps a live pen. The rail resets to a part-1 segment (median 25 s), with the same curve. |
| **Part 1 arrives** | The tab title becomes "Part 1 ready: ..."; the lesson opens | The plate 1 frame expands into the lesson's opening figure (400 ms cross-fade, no zoom). If the warm-up input has focus, show a "Part 1 is ready" button instead of switching. |

Rules: tick only on real events (`progress`, `plan`, `scene`). Nothing loops faster than 1 Hz near text. Total motion stays inside the figure box, and the heading and copy never move.

## 8. Accessibility requirements

- **Status, not a ticker.** One `role="status"` element (implicitly `aria-live="polite"`, `aria-atomic="true"` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Accessibility/ARIA/Reference/Roles/status_role))) holds the stage sentence only. It announces "Planning the lesson", then "Outline ready: 4 parts", then "Part 1 is ready". The elapsed counter sits outside it. WCAG 4.1.3 notes the risk of making an application "too 'chatty' for a screen reader user" ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/status-messages.html)). Today's `LiveLesson.tsx` breaks this: the seconds live inside both `aria-live` sections.
- **Progress bar semantics.** The rail is an estimate, so expose it as `role="progressbar"` *without* `aria-valuenow` (indeterminate), with `aria-valuetext` such as "Planning, usually about 40 seconds". Alternatively, hide it and let the status text carry the meaning. Set `aria-busy="true"` on the region being filled ([MDN progressbar](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Accessibility/ARIA/Reference/Roles/progressbar_role)).
- **The drawing is decorative.** Give it `aria-hidden="true"`; the work list and plates are real text.
- **Pause, Stop, Hide.** SC 2.2.2 covers motion that "(1) starts automatically, (2) lasts more than five seconds, and (3) is presented in parallel with other content". A preload animation can be "essential" only "if interaction cannot occur during that phase" ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). The warm-up makes interaction possible, so ship a visible "Pause drawing" toggle.
- **Reduced motion.** Under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)), show each sketch fully drawn and swap them by a 300 ms opacity fade every 10 s. The rail fills in steps without ribbing, and the plates appear without a morph. This matches the PRD's "instant (fades allowed)" rule and WCAG 2.3.3 ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)).
- **Focus.** Never move focus on stage changes. When part 1 opens automatically, keep focus where it was. The warm-up is a native radio group with a visible label.

## 9. Unverified or open

- The NN/g-cited "3 times longer" Nebraska study; the original was not traced.
- The Bill Chung skeleton study and the Mejtoft et al. effect size: secondary or abstract only.
- The exact wording of the OpenAI deep research help page, the Perplexity Pro Search blog and the Bolt preview docs: my fetcher got HTTP 403 or 404, and I relied on search summaries.
- Tan & Nov 2026 is a preprint; I read the abstract only.
- Buell, Kim & Tsay 2017 and the 2021 MSOM paper: abstracts only.
- Whether a single pretest item during a 30-50 s wait gives a measurable benefit in this app; the lab studies used longer materials and delayed tests.
- That an accelerating, ribbed or pulsing *drawing* (rather than a bar) inherits the Harrison effects. This is an extrapolation.
- Whether the planner could stream outline titles before it finishes (it would shrink the dead zone more than any animation). This was not investigated, and it is a backend question.
